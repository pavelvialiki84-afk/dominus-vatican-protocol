Who Owns the Truth? ## On archives, testimony, artificial intelligence, and the power to close a case

An essay by Pavel Velichii · November 2026

A document survives the death of its author. A signature remains legible long after the hand that wrote it is dust. In the silence of an archive, this can feel like a victory over time.

But there is a troubling second question. What happens when someone else is authorized to say what the document means?

Every archive is built on distinctions. A record is not the event it describes. A witness's account is not the whole of the witnessed life. A catalog number can establish where a document belongs, but it cannot settle every dispute about what happened. Preserving evidence is one task; assigning its meaning is another.

That distinction becomes urgent when institutions — legal, religious, governmental or technological — claim the right to turn an interpretation into a definitive record.

A witness is not a database

A remarkable share of what we know depends on testimony. Most of us cannot directly inspect every historical event, scientific experiment, or reported injustice. We rely on people who tell us what they saw, read, measured, or remember. Philosophers of testimony ask under what conditions another person's words justify belief. They do not treat every assertion as automatically true merely because it has been recorded.

This difficulty does not disappear when testimony becomes searchable text. A transcript can be accurate while omitting hesitation, context, coercion or a correction made later. A digitized record can be perfectly preserved and still be interpreted badly.

Consider an ordinary institutional file. One line states a conclusion. Another, written weeks later, disputes it. If a software system presents only the first sentence to a reader, the extracted words may be authentic — and the picture false.

The danger is not necessarily fabricated data. Sometimes it is the authority attached to a partial account.

The architecture of an archive

The Vatican Apostolic Archive is a real scholarly institution, not a fictional chamber in which everything is concealed. It publishes admission rules for qualified researchers and identifies which historical holdings are open for consultation. Its current access guidance indicates that researchers may consult documents through the end of the pontificate of Pius XII in October 1958.

Even its history illustrates the difference between a name and an interpretation. The former title “Vatican Secret Archive” used an historical term meaning something reserved or set apart. The institution explains that the name changed in 2019 to “Vatican Apostolic Archive.” Reading the word secret through a modern conspiracy-thriller lens would produce a very different impression from the historical terminology.

Real historical work requires exactly this sort of restraint: a term must be read in context, a document checked against its provenance, and an institutional account distinguished from evidence.

Fiction can use the tension. Responsible fiction should not confuse it with an actual claim about the institution.

When an algorithm delivers a verdict

An artificial-intelligence system can summarize texts, identify patterns and produce plausible reconstructions. Those abilities may assist research. They do not, by themselves, confer moral authority to define the meaning of a human life.

This is more than a literary concern. UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence places human dignity, oversight and accountability at the center of responsible governance. The argument is not that computers must never assist decisions. It is that responsibility cannot simply be transferred to a system because its output sounds confident.

Imagine an institution asking a machine to reconstruct testimony from fragmented records. The machine offers a coherent statement. The statement is useful, perhaps even persuasive. An official copies it into a permanent file. Another official quotes it. In time, the reconstruction is no longer introduced as a reconstruction.

It has become the record.

At which precise moment did provisional information become an authoritative judgment? Who could challenge it? What if the person most affected is unable to speak — or is no longer alive?

These are not problems solved by giving the system a name or a human-sounding voice. They are problems of procedure, legitimacy and power.

The right to correct the record

An honest archive protects its sources. An honest investigation also preserves uncertainty: conflicting evidence, disputed readings, inconvenient amendments.

The ability to correct an account matters because people are more than the claims made about them. A final report may be necessary for a court, an institution or a historian. But every conclusion should remain accountable to the methods by which it was reached.

A machine may detect a contradiction. A person or institution must still answer for what follows.

This is the fault line explored in DOMINUS — The Vatican Protocol, Book One: Schism: a fictional investigation where records, human testimony and computational reconstruction collide with institutional authority. The story does not ask readers merely to wonder whether a machine can imitate a voice. It asks what happens when others decide to obey that voice.
