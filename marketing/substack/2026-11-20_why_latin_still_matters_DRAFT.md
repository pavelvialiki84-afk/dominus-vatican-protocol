# Why Latin Still Matters in an Age of Artificial Intelligence
Author: Pavel Velichii
Status: EDITORIAL DRAFT — NOT PUBLISHED
Channel: Substack
Campaign: DOMINUS, research-led author voice
Target: readers of intellectual speculative fiction and historical inquiry

**The machine speaks Latin. No one programmed it to.**

The line belongs to the world of my novel *DOMINUS — The Vatican Protocol*. It also introduces a more interesting question than whether a computer can produce a sentence in an ancient language.

Why would we be disturbed by the language of a machine?

A modern language model can write in Latin, translate a medieval sentence and reproduce the conventions of a theological argument. This does not establish that it understands what it says in the way a human scholar does. Still less does it confer any kind of spiritual or legal authority upon the system. Competence in a language, interpretation of its content and the right to make a binding decision are three different things.

## A language that carries institutions

Latin outlived the political order with which it is most readily associated. It became a language of teaching, law, correspondence, philosophy, liturgy and administrative record-keeping across much of Europe. One encounters it not only in polished literary works but in wills, court records, catalogues, ecclesiastical acts and documents written by people engaged in very practical disputes.

This is part of the reason Latin fascinates me as a researcher. The grammatical surface can be remarkably economical while the historical meaning is not. Two documents may use similar formulas and yet perform different functions. A word that appears straightforward in a dictionary may acquire a specialised meaning in the context of a legal procedure or an institution.

An old text is never merely a string of characters. It has material form, a date or a range of possible dates, an author or issuing authority, a chain of custody and an intended audience. The historical task is to reconstruct these relationships as carefully as the surviving evidence permits.

## Fluency is not provenance

Imagine finding a document that appears to have been written centuries ago. Its Latin is convincing. Its formulas are plausible. It mentions names and titles that fit the period.

That would not, by itself, make the document authentic.

A historian would want to understand where it came from, how it was preserved, whether the handwriting and material support fit the proposed date, and whether the document can be situated among independent evidence. A convincing text can still be copied, altered, misdated or fabricated.

This is where a literary question becomes a contemporary one. Artificial intelligence can generate language that *looks* like an authoritative record. Yet a model's ability to reproduce a form is not proof of the historical event that form describes.

The distinction is easily lost in an age of instantaneous answers.

## The temptation to mistake a voice for authority

When a person speaks in the language of a great institution, listeners may instinctively associate that language with the institution's legitimacy. The effect is partly rhetorical, partly cultural. It does not disappear when the voice comes from a machine.

In the Catholic tradition, for example, canon law is not simply impressive terminology. It is a normative system with institutions, offices, competencies, procedures and rights. A machine that can describe or analyse a procedure does not automatically acquire the competence to perform the legal act the procedure governs.

This distinction—between producing information and possessing legitimate authority—is central to *DOMINUS*.

My interest is not to present AI as a miracle or a demon. Either description would make the story too easy. I am interested in the moment when humans must decide what they will believe about a system, and why. A sentence uttered by a machine may be technically explicable and psychologically devastating at the same time.

## Why fiction?

An archive can preserve evidence. A scholarly article can analyse it. A novel can place a character in the moment when that evidence appears to demand action.

Fiction lets us explore the pressure that institutions and individuals experience when knowledge is incomplete, decisions are urgent and the consequences may be irreversible.

That is the world I wanted to explore in *DOMINUS — The Vatican Protocol*: a world where archives, theological questions and artificial intelligence meet, and where the most dangerous claim may be the claim to speak for someone else.

The machine speaks Latin. The unanswered question is who will decide what its words mean.
