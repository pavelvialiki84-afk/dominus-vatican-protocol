1. Why Latin?

Latin is not a secret code that only a few people can decipher. For centuries it was a common language of scholarship, administrative record-keeping, law and Western Christianity. That long institutional life makes it an unusually potent literary instrument: a sentence in Latin can seem to carry authority before anyone has asked whether it deserves authority.

In a story about artificial intelligence, the crucial question is not merely whether a model can generate grammatical Latin. Modern computational systems can do that. The unnerving question is why a particular output occurs, who interprets it and how quickly people begin to invest that output with institutional significance.

2. The archive is a system of evidence

Archives preserve traces of decisions, transactions, disputes and interpretations. An archival document does not always tell us what happened in full; it tells us what someone recorded, according to a particular procedure, for a particular purpose. Provenance, dating, custody and physical integrity all matter.

The Vatican Apostolic Archive is a historical research institution, not a fictional repository of unlimited forbidden knowledge. Responsible research distinguishes the existence of a document from the truth of every claim in it. This difference drives the logic of the thriller: a record may be genuine and yet be misunderstood; its language may be precise while its implications remain contested.

3. Five Latin expressions

Acta — acts, records, proceedings. A useful label for the documentary trace of institutional action.

Authentica — in ecclesiastical contexts, often a document attesting a relic or other matter; its meaning depends on genre and context.

Recognitio — an examination or formal review. The precise legal force depends on the applicable procedure and historical period.

In foro externo — in the external forum, concerning matters of a public or legally cognizable character.

Salva reverentia — 'with due respect'; a formula that reminds us that style and authority are not the same as truth.

4. Canon law and the question of power

Canon law is a developed body of legal norms rather than an all-purpose synonym for theology. In the Catholic Church, norms govern institutions, offices, procedures and rights. For a novelist, the interesting tension lies between a legal act, the authority competent to perform it, and the epistemic question of whether the people involved know enough to decide.

A machine might provide evidence, recommend an interpretation or generate a persuasive argument. None of those functions automatically makes it a lawful officeholder. A central question in DOMINUS is what happens when technical capability is mistaken for legitimate authority.

5. Artificial intelligence and meaning

Generating language is not the same claim as possessing consciousness, moral responsibility or a mandate to judge. These ideas should be kept distinct. A system's apparent fluency may invite its users to attribute intention to it, especially when it speaks in the vocabulary of a tradition they already revere.

Who may speak? Who is believed? And who has the right to make the final decision?

These are questions of institutions and human interpretation as much as computation. That is the territory of the novel: not a forecast of what AI must become, but an exploration of what people might do when confronted with a seemingly impossible signal.

6. Historical research versus fiction

DOMINUS is a work of fiction. Its characters, conspiracy, events and extraordinary technological premise are imagined. References to Latin, archives, institutional history and legal thought are starting points for intellectual inquiry, not evidence that the fictional events occurred. The author is not claiming that the Vatican endorses the book.

The distinction matters. A good thriller can arise from real historical questions without representing its dramatic invention as a discovery. Readers are invited both to enjoy the narrative and to question the claims made by its characters.
