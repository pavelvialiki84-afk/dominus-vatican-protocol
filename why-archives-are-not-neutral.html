Why Archives Are Not Neutral

By Pavel Velichii · 20 November 2026 · An original essay on the themes behind the novel

The record that survived

A surviving document feels objective because it is tangible. Paper has weight. A handwritten signature occupies a place on the page. Dates, stamps and annotations invite verification. Yet none of these details tells us why this record survived while countless others disappeared.

Letters are discarded, collections are reorganized, inventories are rewritten and files sometimes remain inaccessible. Archives are not therefore worthless or untrustworthy. Their existence is often the product of extraordinary care. But their holdings reflect decisions about what deserved preservation.

A catalog label is an interpretation

Imagine one record described as a confession and another cataloging system describing it as an interview. The underlying pages have not changed. The vocabulary around them has. A researcher searching only for one term may never encounter the other.

Titles, names, dates and subject headings are indispensable tools. They are also the results of conventions and judgments that can change over time. Good archival practice makes those judgments traceable and open to revision rather than pretending they never happened.

The limits of a perfect search

Now imagine an artificial intelligence searching thousands of collections, translating documents and drawing connections in seconds. Such tools can reveal patterns that human readers would otherwise miss. That possibility matters.

Yet a system can search only the material it can access. It can inherit an incorrect attribution, overvalue the cleanest surviving version or silently omit a relevant but uncataloged witness. An elegant answer can hide the absences on which it was built.

Reading against the grain

Before accepting a confident archival conclusion, ask what would have to be missing for it to be wrong. Which voices were excluded? Who defined the catalog categories? Does the answer distinguish a record from the event it describes? Can a later reader challenge its interpretation?

These questions do not weaken respect for evidence. They strengthen it. A record is not the final word on a life. It is an invitation to investigate.

From history to fiction

In DOMINUS — The Vatican Protocol. Book One: Schism, archival uncertainty becomes the foundation of a suspense story involving memory, artificial intelligence and institutional authority. The novel is fiction; the historical methods that inspire its questions are real.

An archive never speaks entirely by itself. Someone opens the box, reads the page and decides what to say next.
