Can a Machine Speak for a Witness?

By Pavel Velichii · 20 November 2026 · An original essay on the themes behind the novel

The seduction of a complete record

Imagine a recording damaged beyond ordinary repair. Fragments of speech remain under layers of noise. A new system separates the voices, restores the missing syllables and produces a transcript that looks complete. The result is impressive. It can also be dangerously persuasive.

A clean transcript removes the visible signs of uncertainty that existed in the source. Readers see a seamless statement instead of a reconstruction. They may forget how much inference sits between the evidence and its apparently final form.

Accuracy is not authorization

A reconstructed statement can help an investigation or allow a family to hear a voice that seemed lost. Yet accuracy only describes how closely a reconstruction fits the available evidence. It does not prove that the person being represented authorized that reconstruction to speak in their name.

A witness may hesitate, withdraw an accusation, contradict an earlier account or decide that a private thought should remain private. People correct themselves. They grow. These changes are not defects to be repaired by a more capable model. They belong to a person's continuing agency.

Who gives a machine authority?

The danger does not require a conscious machine. It begins when officials, readers or institutions confer authority on an output that was meant to be provisional. A probability becomes a quotation. A quotation becomes a finding. A finding becomes a verdict.

That chain consists of human decisions. Every step should be documented, challengeable and open to correction. Transparency is not merely a feature of software; it is a discipline imposed on the people who use it.

The question behind DOMINUS

DOMINUS — The Vatican Protocol. Book One: Schism turns the tension between testimony and authority into the engine of an intellectual thriller. Its events and extraordinary technological premise are fictional, not claims about actual Vatican systems.

The question beneath the suspense is unsettlingly simple: when evidence can outlive its maker, who has the right to decide what it means? We may learn to recover lost voices. We must be careful not to confuse recovering a voice with owning it.
